Why markup and margin are not the same number

Both describe the same profit, but they measure it against different things. Markup divides by your cost. Margin divides by your selling price. Because the denominators differ, the two numbers always diverge, and the gap grows as your margin rises.

A 40% markup sounds healthy. It is actually a 28.6% margin, because you are measuring the profit against the larger number. This is the single most common pricing mistake small businesses make: they quote a healthy-sounding markup and then discover their real margin cannot cover overhead.

Markup on costGross marginWhat it means
25%20.0%Very thin, rarely sustainable
40%28.6%Common retail baseline
50%33.3%Healthy distribution business
100%50.0%Doubling cost, standard in services
200%66.7%Strong services or software
300%75.0%Very high, rare in goods resale

To convert between them:

  • Markup to margin: margin = markup ÷ (1 + markup)
  • Margin to markup: markup = margin ÷ (1 − margin)

So if you need a 30% gross margin to survive your overhead, you have to quote a 42.9% markup. That is the number most people forget to check before they raise a price.

Common questions

What is the difference between markup and margin?

Markup is calculated on your cost: markup = (price − cost) ÷ cost. Margin is calculated on your revenue: margin = (price − cost) ÷ price. The same product can carry a 40% markup and only a 28.6% margin. Markup is the number most people use when quoting a price. Margin is the number your financial statements, lenders, and buyers actually evaluate.

How do I convert a markup into a margin?

Divide the markup by one plus the markup. A 40% markup becomes 28.6%, a 100% markup becomes 50%, a 200% markup becomes 66.7%. Going the other way, divide the target margin by one minus the margin: a 30% target margin needs a 42.9% markup.

Should I price by markup or by margin?

Use markup when you set prices from cost, because it is intuitive for most products and services. Use margin when you are testing whether a price survives your overhead, or when you are comparing yourself to a competitor, because margin decides whether a sale is profitable at all. In practice you need both: markup to set the price, margin to check whether that price is safe.

Does a higher markup always mean a better business?

No. A high markup on a slow-moving product can produce less total profit than a modest markup on something you sell ten times as often. Gross margin tells you about the health of a single sale. Turnover and volume determine whether that health becomes an actual income.

What counts as cost of goods for a service business?

Include the direct cost of delivering the work: contractor labor you pay for, materials, subcontractor invoices, cloud and software you use per project, and travel specific to a job. Do not include rent, salaries of permanent staff, or general admin, because those belong in operating expenses. Mixing the two is what makes service businesses misjudge their margins.

Should gross margin include shipping and packaging?

It depends on how you report. Many small businesses put packaging into cost of goods and shipping into operating expenses. Others fold both into cost of goods. Pick one method, apply it consistently, and write it down, because mixing the two makes your margins incomparable over time.
